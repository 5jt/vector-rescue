The ISI Dictionary of J¶

J is a dialect of APL, a formal imperative language. Because it is imperative, a sentence in APL may also be called an instruction, and may be executed to produce a result. Because it is formal and unambiguous it can be executed mechanically by a computer, and is therefore called a programming language, a phrase from which its name derives. Because it shares the analytic properties of mathematical notation, it is also called an analytic language.

APL originated in an attempt to provide consistent notation for the teaching and analysis of topics related to the application of computers, and developed through its use in a variety of topics, and through its implementation in computer systems. Discussions of the language may be found in The Design of APL and The Evolution of APL [1]. Reference 2 discusses a freeware implementation of the language defined here, as well as its relation to the earlier version [3], which also provides information on other dialects.

A dictionary should not be read as an introduction to a language, but should rather be consulted in conjunction with other material (such as Reference 4) that uses the language in some context of interest. On the other hand, a dictionary should be used not only to find the meanings of individual words, but should also be studied to gain an overall view of the language.

I: Alphabet and Words¶

The alphabet used is that of ASCII, and the words used to denote facilities in the language are spelled with either one ASCII character or with one character followed by a full stop (.) or a stop (:). Spaces between such words are permitted but not required, except that a number beginning with a decimal point must be prefaced by a space or a zero. The entire spelling scheme is shown in the Language Summary of Table 1.

Pronouns and proverbs begin with a letter of the English alphabet and continue with further letters or digits. Such a name followed by a colon is a distinguished or non-standard word that may, in a particular implementation, be used to supplement the standard words.

Quotes behave much as in English: everything between an opening quote and a closing quote is treated as a single word, except that a pair of adjacent quotes following an opening quote represents the quote character itself; 'can''t' is the five-character abbreviation of the six-character word 'cannot'.

The negative sign is denoted by _, as in _3 and _3.14. The _ alone denotes infinity, and __ denotes negative infinity. A real number may also be denoted in exponential form, as in 3.25e4 to signify 32500. A complex number is denoted by a j between two real numbers, as in _3e4j5.6. Numbers separated by spaces denote a list or vector, as in prices=. 3.12 4.6 2e3.

Verbs act upon nouns to produce noun results; the nouns to which a particular verb apply are called its arguments. A verb may have two distinct (but usually related) meanings according to whether it is applied to one argument (to its right), or to two arguments (left and right). For example, 2%5 yields .4, and %5 yields .2.

An adverb acts upon a single noun or verb (to its left) to produce a verb. For example, +/ is a (derived) verb called plus over that sums an argument list to which it is applied, and */ yields the product of a list. A conjunction applies to two arguments (either nouns or verbs) to produce a verb.

Punctuation is provided by paired parentheses that specify the sequence of execution as in elementary algebra. The word =. behaves like the copulas "is" and "are" and is read as such, as in "area is 3 times 4" for area=. 3*4. The name area thus assigned is a pronoun and, as in English, it plays the role of a noun. Similar remarks apply to names assigned to verbs, adverbs, and conjunctions.

A. Nouns¶

Nouns are classified in three ways: numeric or literal; open or boxed; arrays of various ranks, called (for ranks 0, 1, and 2) atom, list, and table, or (in mathematics) scalar, vector and matrix. Numbers and literals are represented as stated in Part I.

Arrays. A single entity such as 'A' or '+' or 2.3 or _2.3j5 is called an atom. The verb denoted by a comma chains its arguments to form a list whose shape (given by the verb $) is equal to the number of atoms combined. For example:

B. Verbs¶

Monads and Dyads. Most verbs have two definitions, one for the monadic case (one argument), and one for the dyadic case (two arguments). In any sentence, the dyadic definition of a verb applies if it is preceded by a suitable left argument, that is, any noun that is not itself an argument of a conjunction; otherwise the monadic definition applies. The monadic case of a verb is also called a monad, and we speak of the monad % used in the phrase %4, and of the dyad % used in 3%4.

The ranks of a verb merely place upper limits on the ranks of the cells to which it applies, and its domain may include arguments of lower rank. For example, matrix inverse (%.) has monadic rank 2, but applies to degenerate cases of vector and scalar arguments by treating them as one-column matrices.

C. Adverbs and Conjunctions¶

Unlike verbs, adverbs and conjunctions have fixed valence; an adverb is monadic (applying to a single argument to its left), and a conjunction is dyadic.

Each argument of a conjunction may be either a noun or a verb, and it may therefore produce as may as four distinct classes of results. For example, u&v produces the composition of the verbs u and v; ^&2 produces the square by combining the power function with the right argument 2; and 2&^ produces the function 2-to-the-power. The conjunction & may therefore be referred to by different names for the different cases, or it may be referred to by the single term and (or with), which roughly covers all cases.

D. Comparatives¶

The comparison x=y is treated like the everyday use of equality (that is, with a reasonable relative tolerance), yielding 1 if the difference x-y falls relatively close to zero. Tolerant comparison also applies to other relations and to floor and ceiling (<. and >.); a precise definition is given in Part III under the comparative less-than-or-equal (<:). An arbitrary tolerance t can be specified by using the custom conjunction (&:), as in x =&:t y.

E. Parsing and Execution¶

A sentence is evaluated by executing phrases in a sequence determined by the parsing rules of the language. For example, in the sentence 10%3+2, the phrase 3+2 is evaluated first to obtain a result that is then used to divide 10. In summary, the parsing rules are:

  1. Execution proceeds from right to left, except that when a right parenthesis is encountered, the segment enclosed by it and its matching left parenthesis is executed, and its result replaces the entire segment and its enclosing parentheses.
  2. Adverbs and conjunctions are executed before verbs; the phrase ,"2-a is equivalent to (,"2)-a, not to ,"(2-a). Moreover, the left argument of an adverb or conjunction is the entire verb phrase that precedes it. For example, in the phrase a+/..*/b, the rightmost adverb / applies to the verb derived from the phrase +/..*, not to the verb *.
  3. Verbs are applied dyadically if possible. For example, a-b signifies subtraction, but a*-b signifies multiplication of a by the negative of b.
  4. Certain phrases that would be invalid under these rules are treated as idioms, as described in Section F.

One important consequence of these rules is that in an unparenthesized expression the right argument of any verb is the result of the entire phrase to the right of it. The sentence 3*p%q^|r-5 can therefore be read from left to right: the overall result is 3 times the result of the remaining phrase, which is the quotient of p and the part following the %, and so on.

Parsing proceeds by moving successive elements (or their values in the case of pronouns and other names) from the tail end of a queue (initially the original sentence prefixed by a left marker [) to the front of a stack, and eventually executing some eligible portion of the stack and replacing it by the result of the execution.

For example, if a=. 1 2 3, and if ◊ separates the queue and stack, then b=.+/2*a would be parsed and executed as follows:

The foregoing illustrates two major points: 1) Execution of the phrase 2*1 2 3 is deferred until the next element (the /) is transferred; had it been a conjunction, the 2 would have been its argument, and the monad * would have applied to 1 2 3; and 2) Whereas the value of the name a is moved to the stack, the name b (because it precedes a copula) moves unchanged. Finally, a side effect is that the pronoun b is assigned the value 12.

The executions in the stack are confined to the first four elements only, and eligibility for execution is determined only by the class of each of these elements (noun, verb, etc.). The parsing rules are prescribed more formally in Table 2.

The classes of the first four elements of the stack are compared with the first four columns of the parse table, and the first row that agrees in all four columns is selected. The bold elements in the row are then subjected to the action prescribed in column 5, and are replaced by its single result.

F. Idioms¶

Any phrase, such as (+*-), which cannot be evaluated according to the normal grammatical rules, can, without contradiction, be assigned any idiomatic significance desired. The idioms fork and hook are defined by the following diagrams, using f, g, and h to denote functions (i.e., verbs):

        FORK                    HOOK

   g          g            g          g
  / \        / \          / \        / \
 f   h      f   h        y   h      x   h
 |   |     / \ / \           |          |
 y   y    x  y x  y          y          y

For example, 5(+*-)3 is (5+3)*(5-3).

The sixth line of Table 2 shows the conditions for a fork: three verbs in the stack preceded by anything but a conjunction (which would, of course, seize the leading verb as its argument).

Appendix A: System Commands¶

The following commands may be entered at any point in a session; the leading right parenthesis distinguishes them from normal sentences. Brackets indicate that the argument y is optional; when omitted, the present value of the parameter is printed.

A right parenthesis may also be used to specify a label, as described under the conjunction :: (definition).

)box [y]
The eleven-element integer list y specifies the indices of the ASCII characters to be used in displaying boxed arrays, in the order : ┌ ┬ ┐ ├ ┼ ┤ └ ┴ ┘ │ ─
)clear
Restore to initial condition, erasing all names.
)off
Terminate session.
)prompt [y]
The prompt for immediate input is specified by y. The default value is ' ' (that is, three spaces).
)reset
Terminate all suspended functions.
)rl [y]
The "random link" used as a "seed" by the random number generator (?) is specified by y. The default value is 16807.
)script [y]
The argument is one (quoted) file name for input or two (as in 'abc' 'defg') for input and output. Except that system commands are ignored, the entire input file is executed as a script before further input is accepted from the keyboard. An unquoted dash (-) for the input file denotes keyboard input. The output is directed to the screen as well as to any designated output file. An output file so generated is suitable as the input in a subsequent command.
)sscript [y]
Behaves like )script, except that output does not appear on the screen.
)si
Stack Indicator, showing functions currently suspended.
