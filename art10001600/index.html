What, in Computing, is Worth Learning?

Vector 18:3, page 71

education development practice

Transcribed from the printed issue; not yet reviewed. Read it in the PDF of the issue, page 71

by Anthony Camacho

A Statement¶

I started programming in 1958 and was immediately irritated by the programmer habit of calling an instruction a "statement". I have done my best to avoid following this unseemly trend. I think it may be (at least indirectly) responsible for some of the many difficulties we labour under today.

The Imperative Voice¶

When I learned grammar the imperative was the voice used for commands. All communications to a computer are in the imperative voice. Every press of a key or movement of a mouse or click of a mouse button (or movement of a mouse wheel) is a command and, if the computer is working, all it can do is to obey. If we were all always clear that what we are doing when we operate a computer is commanding it to do things then we might take the trouble to find out what the commands actually are and might be less willing to accept the computer man’s starting point (which is "I know better than you what you are trying to do and will make the computer do it my way").

The Will¶

People have wishes; computers do not. Computers may be programmed to pursue an assigned goal and thus mislead the naïve into thinking computers want things. If you can only communicate with another being in the imperative voice, and the only responses are obedient or random, I don’t know whether it would be possible to discern a wish, even allowing for the possibility that a computer can have one (which I emphatically deny).

Thinking¶

Doing logic may be thinking, but if all you can do is logic and if you can only do it when told and with specified premises, then that is probably not thinking. If Babbage’s Analytic Engine had ever been made, its processing could hardly have been called thinking, any more than the Turing machine’s processes. Perhaps it is of the essence of thinking that the train of thought can be interrupted. A computer’s train of thought cannot be interrupted in the sense that an unforeseen idea arising during the logic can divert the train of thought to change its goal. Let me hasten to deny that I am saying that thinking lies in an unpredictable outcome. What I am saying is that if the outcome is completely determined then I doubt if the process is, beyond doubt, "thinking".

‘Helping’ You¶

I now have four grandchildren and am well qualified to appreciate the consequences of accepting an offer of help from any or all of them. The help offered by Microsoft is worse than what my grandchildren can do. At least they make no pretence of trying to further my wishes. Microsoft staff (and not only them) try to help by knowing better than I do what it is I want to do. I cannot begin a paragraph with a number or a bullet without getting all subsequent paragraphs beginning with a number in sequence or a bullet. This is the default behaviour! The time and trouble it takes to stop the program doing this is more than I would have spent in ten years numbering paragraphs by hand.

Concealment¶

In pursuing the aim of making the machine ‘friendly’ to the user, Microsoft have largely hidden from the user, the logical control of the computer. For example on this new computer I have five icons on the desktop that I know offer to get updates, make contacts etc. with the internet. Two of them are the machine manufacturer’s (set up and connect icons) and one is the virus software update but the rest are Microsoft. I don’t have a clear idea what will happen if I click on one of these. I have not entered any telephone numbers, so I don’t know what will be dialled. For all I know the call will be made to a call centre in Hong Kong. I can check my telephone bill on calls I dial or when I have entered the number into the computer, but not when I use these icons. Furthermore there are programs that run when my machine first boots up that I do not want to run and that I cannot find out how to stop.

When Things Go Wrong¶

In particular I haven’t any way of telling how to correct things if something goes wrong. When I clicked the option on the virus software window to create a rescue disk it told me that Rescue 32 had executed an invalid instruction in module KRNL386.EXE at 0001:00002d51 and when I clicked the box to continue I was shown a blue screen with "A fatal exception OE has occurred at 0187:BFF9DFFF. The current application will be terminated". There is no way of telling what went wrong. When I learned about planning, I was taught that a good plan was one which allowed for all eventualities; it foresaw that there would be difficulties and provided some way of overcoming them, even if there was no easy way of knowing precisely what kind of difficulties they would be. The current machine philosophy is to plan for everything going right first time and, if things don’t, tough!

No Manuals¶

The replacement of manuals (which required that someone made an intellectual effort to organise the information logically) by help files (which don’t) has been a disaster. The typical help file begins by assuming that you need to be told what options there are in the menu on the screen and what each of them does. If what you need is not allowed for then there is nothing more you can do with it. It is possible that I have forgotten it if I have ever found something in a help file that was helpful but I certainly can remember many occasions when I didn’t. It is not even possible to find out what files provide which facilities. On my other machine the scroll bar sometimes fails: the symptom is that scrolling begins and continues whenever the mouse is anywhere on the bar. Furthermore the direction of scrolling varies. I suspect that if I knew which .DLL or .VXD file provided the scroll bars I could reload it from a Windows Cabinet and my troubles would be over – but there is no practical way of finding out. There is a horrible tendency for the style to creep out of the machine onto paper. Some of the books I have recently bought follow the help file pattern: they propose a sequence of steps (all expressed in the imperative mood) which the author thinks will work (and maybe does on his machine) but without any allowance for the steps that don’t work as expected. Imagine a motor manual which tried to tell how to diagnose and repair an internal combustion engine entirely by reference to the driver’s controls.

Divide and Rule¶

I buy a first class machine from one of the most reputable makers. They send it with the wrong network card installed. After three weeks of ringing them and emailing them and even writing letters of complaint to the sales director, I get a network card with a plug to suit my network wiring. Then, when I set it up according to the instructions, it doesn’t work as it should. To begin with it can only see one other machine on the network. Then after trying so many things that I cannot report them coherently to the help line, it gets to see three machines. The fourth (which the others can all see and share files with) is still invisible. The help line politely declines to help saying that the manufacturer cannot solve network problems as too much is outside their control. Microsoft too decline to help on the grounds that their arrangement with the manufacturer requires the manufacturer to provide operating system support. The network card manufacturer’s email help service sends me a standard general purpose debugging guide which tells me that I have to specify a machine name and the correct network name (I had, of course, told them that the rest of the network was working and that the problem machine could see one of the other machines: if they had bothered to read what I wrote they would have known I had done all that.) and ends "NOTE: for more information on peer to peer networking in Microsoft Windows 95/98 go to Microsoft’s website or contact Microsoft’s Technical support".

Parrot Learning¶

Help files and help lines are, as described above, completely hooked on giving sequences of instructions. They almost never explain what the instructions command the machine to do. When they do give a purpose for an action it is usually an anthropomorphic one: "This tells the machine where to find the XX file." On one occasion, when I was asking a help line why my instructions were not adequate to make my machine do what I wanted, the guy on the other end was telling me, one step at a time what to do and I recognised ‘DELTREE’ and asked him what he was doing. After a little gentle verbal fencing he admitted that he was intending to re-install my Windows system from scratch! He hadn’t asked me. He hadn’t explained to me the consequences of such an action. Such people don’t. This approach conceals from people the nature of their actions. I don’t know where to find the other approach; one that assumes the user knows what he wants and explains the principles underlying the steps needed to get it and maybe even the main things that could go wrong and how to overcome them.

Subordinate Learning¶

It is the consequences of all these things that make me deeply suspicious of the value of teaching IT (information technology – it doesn’t deserve its capital letters) in schools. Mainly, what people learn is how to operate pieces of software. Doing this is mainly a process of learning, parrot fashion, the sequences of mouse movements and clicks to do some simple task such as mailing a letter to all the people on an address list. Then the pupil goes away and discovers that, on the next machine he tries, the software is entirely different and the painfully acquired parrot learning is largely useless. (Actually the varieties of word processors and spreadsheets are so similar in their aims and in the ways the computer can be programmed to provide the commands that a good deal of the range of commands is re-usable.) Nevertheless it is a grave defect of computer learning that what is taught is the parrot-fashion sequences needed to operate a piece of software that will almost certainly be superseded before the pupil has even left school. I once worked out that I am now on my ninth word processor, counting the four versions of Word for Windows as one, and most of the effort of learning them was wasted.

A Profession?¶

The mark of a profession is that its respected practitioners know what they are doing and can tell how much work they have to do to complete a task or a stage in the work. In some professions, such as building, there are even people (quantity surveyors) whose job is to measure how much is done and how much there is to do. There is no equivalent of a quantity surveyor in computing. Very few programmers can keep to their estimates. Even fewer are secure enough to dare to offer fixed prices (or schedules) for the work. It is the norm that computer projects are delivered late and cost more than estimated. It is even quite common for a project to get abandoned after large expenditure because the budget holder belatedly realises that he is unable to control the work he wants done. The mark of a professional programmer is that he will do a fixed price job once the specification is agreed. There are very few such people. Of course it is partly the problems described above which put people off. If I knew that I would be able to get Windows to do what I wanted I would be able to give a fixed price for the whole job. Since I may have to spend a week (no exaggeration) getting a new machine to communicate over the network, I have to include rather large safety margins if the estimate is to include getting a program to work under Windows. So does everyone else.

Worth Learning?¶

It would be worth learning how to produce a reliable estimate. It would be worth learning how to regain control of the machine from the operating system. What will continue to be relevant in ten years is more worth learning than anything that will be superseded. It is not easy to distinguish between these.

Hardware Changes¶

When I started programming the IBM 650 (the first commercial computer of which 1000 were sold) I refused to believe that the program I wrote had run correctly because there was no pause between reading the punched cards containing my first program and the punching out of the result. I was wrong; the result was correct. (Incidentally that may have been the last program I wrote that ran first time, error free.) The 650 had a word time of 0.96 milliseconds; each location on the drum became available once every 48 milliseconds and if the locations of addends and result on the drum were optimised an addition could be done in about 7 milliseconds. The drum stored 2000 ten-digit numbers. The machine I am typing this on has a word time of a nanosecond and 128 million bytes of memory available at about three nanoseconds plus forty thousand million bytes on a disk available 4096 bytes at a time with a delay of under ten milliseconds. Its operations are a million times faster, its access to data about ten thousand times faster and it can store at least two million times as much, so overall it is about sixteen orders of magnitude more capable. The IBM 650 cost £75,000 in 1958 money – say the equivalent of £3,000,000 to £4,000,000 today.

Software Changes¶

Much as the hardware has changed, I think the software has changed more, but the measures to judge it by are not so easy to see. The hardware does the same things, but faster and with more data; the software now handles pictures and music and video and the interface with the user is completely different. The instruction codes, the utilities, the calls to the operating system, all have been changed and the operating system is now huge. Gone are the days when one could blow one 4K ROM chip and have a reasonable debugger available on command. Debug, NasDis (disassembler) and Zeap (Z80 assembler) all fit into 8 chips. Nowadays even Visual BASIC takes two 1.44 Mbyte disks. The Windows operating system nowadays uses 90% or more of the machine resources!

For some years one could rely on DOS doing what it used to do the year before. The calls to read and write a file remained unchanged for many years. Now DOS features that Windows used to support are being phased out, even this reliance is shaky. I am told that I can no longer (in Windows ME or 2000) do the things I used to do in CONFIG.SYS and AUTOEXEC.BAT. Not only that: there is no longer a way to do all of those things!

What Can’t Change?¶

The problem is to discern the stable features of the landscape. What is there that one can rely on staying the same?

Computers will always be slaves¶

A computer is an algorithmic machine: all communications to it are in the imperative voice. This will still be true and there will still be programs in ten years. The programming languages then in use will be those that have proved themselves suitable. Perhaps, by then, people will have ceased to treat Edsger Dijkstra’s view that learning BASIC causes brain damage as a clever-clever joke and begun to realise that it is true. Some of today’s programs will still be running then, of course, and so some of the programming languages you can learn today will still be saleable skills in ten and maybe even twenty years.

All computers will ever hold is a binary representation¶

A computer is a binary machine: everything it stores must be converted to and from a binary representation. The representations of symbols, values and pictures will still have to be translated to and from binary. The established representations will doubtless be joined by others, but are themselves unlikely to be superseded. It is interesting to consider how long IBM’s double precision 64-bit floating point numbers will be the primary scientific numerical representation and whether what supersedes them will just have greater precision or have a variable precision (maybe perfect – without any tolerance arising from the representation at all). One programming language already provides this. It is called J (not Java).

Computer data will always be held in files¶

Information will continue to be held in files and file handling will continue to be a major part of data processing. It may be that some file types become established as the most suitable for the kind of data to be held. Typefaces, for example, which were once held as bit maps will plainly never be held that way again; they are held as the curves of their outlines so that there are no limits on enlargement and reduction. It would be a surprise if the multiplicity of formats for pictures doesn’t shake out a bit in ten years. Word 2000, when asked, offers to import a picture from any of about 35 different file formats.

Non-computer people will always be responsible for the work¶

The jobs computers do (including personal computers) will still be the responsibility of the people that bear the responsibility for seeing that the work gets done and not of the programmers and analysts that design the computer systems. Although programmers and analysts are usually paid more, an application’s success depends on how well it meets the needs of the users. It is a common fault that the system only does what the analyst thinks the user wants. Success sometimes depends on the user feeling a commitment to make a system work and this is unusual unless the user has fully understood and agreed to the design. This ought to be obvious (but see HMW’s sustaining news in Vector 17.4). Companies ought to put users in charge of the computer people to ensure that the system design can do the job and that the change-over procedure is practical and offers fall-back at every stage. Nowadays the fact that computer people are more highly paid often means that their convenience overrides the convenience of the people responsible for the work. This is just one of the many reasons why projects fail.

The user will always have second thoughts¶

It will still be true that one has to walk before one can run. New systems should be introduced partially or on samples of the work and proved to be satisfactory before gradually taking over the whole of the work. Pilot running is better than parallel running and if the system, at the pilot stage, can be changed to incorporate every improvement that the responsible staff can see, so much the better.

So we need ways to run pilot systems with prototype programs that can easily be amended to incorporate the improvements that are only thought of when the staff have some experience with the pilot.

The big problem will always be to discern the real need . . . .¶

Users and computer people can easily be misled (mislead themselves – mislead each other) and agree to designs that don’t work when installed. Far too little effort is made by managers to ensure that their staff can use the system they agree to. When the staff try the prototype, listen to what they say: if changes are not needed then the system is exceptional and the designer deserves a bonus.

. . . . and to agree how the system will meet it – an agreement that will stick¶

Even if the staff have agreed and signed the design in blood, the system may need to be changed. There is no escaping the need for a prototype. To meet these last two needs calls for the skill to convert between expressions of the users’ needs and expressions of the systems design. The field specifications and the range of values each can take and the screen designs and how the user will interact with each of them have to be agreed with the user and the prototype changed in the light of experience in use. The structure of files, of processes, of validations, of file amendments, of back-up arrangements, of security and of response times are all things the designer should handle without confusing the user.

The way these aspects are expressed in the design may make a significant difference in the difficulty of translating the constraints and benefits of a particular design into expressions the user can appreciate.

Conventional wisdom has it that the benefit of a high level language is that it relieves the programmer of much of the drudgery in converting the program specification into machine code and of course it does do this. Systems don’t usually get into difficulties because the program doesn’t meet the program specification. The usual problem is that the delivered system doesn’t meet the real-world need.

Actually the main benefit of a high level language is the same benefit A. N. Whitehead claimed for a good notation:

By relieving the brain of all unnecessary work, a good notation sets it free to concentrate on more advanced problems, and in effect increases the mental power of the race.1
