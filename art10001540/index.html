Editorial: Monsieur Langlet s Enterprise by Anthony Camacho

The philosophyof science is the study of how it is that we discover the truth about the world, and in particular those generaltruthsthat allow usto foresee, to someextent, the consequencesof our actions. Without such foresight morality is impossible since morality consists in acting so as to avoid doing harm. This showsthat knowledge is primary. The standard approachto the problem of discovering laws about the world is to try to guess at what might be a law and thentry to disproveit. If serious, well thoughtout attempts to disprove a hypothetical law havefailed to find fault then we may acceptit. Mostscientific experiments are involvedin these attempts to disprove a hypothesis.

The hypothesis is, of course, a model. It is a symbolic description of the world andcarries withit, in the meaning of its symbols, the clue to what tests would disproveit.

As I understand it Gérard Langlet, apart from his other work, suggests that, at the lowest level and thefinest detail, the way the world works must be simple. Complexity can only arise with structure and structureis built of parts which are combinations of the lowest level and finest details.

If the physical world is composed of quanta and timetoo is non-continuous then the models of the world which assume a continuum are inappropriate for dealing with quantised time and space.

Gérard suggests that, instead of forming our hypothetical models to match the world, we should build upstructures from the simplest possible elements bits. Whentheseare elaborated to the level where the real-world counterparts could be observed then wecan seek for such real world counterparts. A perfect match, that defies all attempts at disproof, between such a model and the real world would bea real breakthrough. J am not sure howclose the APL Theory of Human Vision gets: getting close enoughto consider the questionis exciting enough.

Dyadic Systems Ltd Dyadic is extremely pleased with the way the new version of Dyalog APL was received at APL94. Delegates who attended Dyadic s Vendor Forum (and most did) heard John Scholes give the first presentation of Namespaces outside the UK. The reaction from the audience, most of whom were unawareof this new development, was extremely positive.

Namespacesallow you to organise your functions and variables into logical units and sub-systems, just as the DOS. directory structureallows you to organise your files. In Dyalog APL, the active workspace is also knownas the root namespace; ratherlike the root directory in DOS. Objects that you create at this level are held in the root namespace,justlike files in your root directory. (If you don t want to use namespaces, you candefine all of your objects in the root namespace, just as before.) However, you can also create a namespace (analogous to a sub- directory) in which you can place other objects and indeed other namespaces. There is no limit to the number of namespaces you can have, nor to the depth they are nested.

Namespaces do not just allow you to organise your workspacebetter, they also act as different contexts for the execution of code. When a function runs in a namespace, the namesof variables and other functions to whichit refers are local to that namespace, and do notconflict with objects with the same namesin other namespacesor in the outer workspace. This meansthat,for the first time in APL, you can write a self-contained utility or a complete application that can safely be copied into any other workspace and run without fear of name conflicts. Namespaces also make it extremely easy to implement large application workspaces that contain logically separate sub-systems, without the need to resort to overlay files and similar techniques.

Namespaces providebenefits to all APL developers, but are of particular appeal to those developing GUI applications. This is because GUI objects (Forms, Buttons, Grids and so forth) are also namespaces themselves. This means that you can store all of the callback functions, and any data variables that they require, inside the object to which they apply, rather than having them swill around in the workspace. Namespaces provide a much needed solution for the requirement to pass information between callback functions, and between

successive invocations of the samecallback, without the use of global variables (ie. variables that are global to the workspace as a whole). Instead, such variables becomestatic variables, that are local to an object (and thus directly accessible from functions running within that object) but do not pollute the workspace as a whole. Furthermore, as GUI objects are namespaces, you can write a completely self-contained GUI widget that containsall the required APL code and data, together with its GU] components, and save it as a single object that can subsequently be copied into any active workspace and run. The user sees only whatthe object does; not how it doesit, a fundamental tenet of object- oriented programming.

Dyadic intends to use namespacesasthe basis of further development towards object-oriented programming, and to provide the advantages (but not the complexities) of this approach for Dyalog APLusers in the future.

Editorial by Ian Clark

Welcometo another edition of Education VECTOR.This goesto press just before APL 94, so whatever enlightenment emerges during the symposium will just haveto wait for the next issue.

A teacherfriend I saw recently congratulated herself on getting through thefirst week of term. Her face wasn t grey and the lines on it were fading (I thought) so she must have had a good summerholiday. With the unions all set to continue their boycott of the compulsory testing of the National Curriculum and the Governmentstill striving to re-introducethe eleven-plus in one form or another, with amateur markers to swell the ranks of amateur inspectors, no doubt all riding to work on trains directed by amateur volunteer signalmen, this has got to be the Golden Age of the Amateur Volunteer.

Nowhereis this more evident than the teaching of Mathematics. A recent report on the sorry state of mathematics teaching seemsto lay the blame squarely on Primary Schools. Primary teachers might justifiably wonder what it is the country really wants of them. To be the saviours of the next generation? Or to serve as whipping boysfor the undesirable side-effects of a society governed by marketforces and other gods of darkness? If mathematical skills are to be costed on their current market value by sector,is it any surprise that they re tending to accumulate in the faster end ofthe financial sector and desert the junior end (and upwards)of the educational sector?

For years those further up the educational tree have been telling the primary schools thatall they require of them are children able to go to thetoilet without supervision, tie their own shoelaces and write their own names. Leavethe fancy stuff to those better qualified to teach it. Now it emerges that primary teachers have to carry the can back for the state of Mathematics throughoutthe entire system.

This isn t the place to discuss the role of specialist teaching in primary schools. Nor the evidence for and against the existence of things called pre-mathematical skills. But I do think that those responsible for the staffing and remuneration of schools should not hide from criticism behind the honest attempts of others to putinto effect the results of their own loose thinking. I rememberseeing a report, at least six or seven years ago now, aboutthe teaching of Mathematics in both primary and secondary schools in England and Wales. It furnished figures to

show that at least 70% of mathematics teachers had no recognisable qualifications in mathematics beyond O-level, and that perhaps 90% were teaching the subject far abovethe level at which they were technically qualified.

Now never mind the reasons for this state of affairs and why it cannot be otherwise. Never mind asking whether they are doing a good job. Never mind wondering how to compel them to do a better one, by performancerelated pay, say, or testing their pupils at frequent intervals with nationwide tests, The question we wantto ask ourselves is no, not whether we're feeling lucky, but whether we would tolerate such a situation in the teaching of French, say, or Spanish.

Now I have a shameful admission to make. | hardly know a word of Spanish (except one or two I learnt from reading Hemingway, which it seems I don t pronounce correctly anyway). Yet, if Spanish were to be treated as Mathematics is at present, I could be recruited to teach PE, RE or any other E for which I mightbe qualified, then directed to teach Spanish throughoutthe school. Would any blameattach to meif someofficial Spaniard came along and castigated my teaching(1 think that s the word)? In a fair and just society, notatall. The blame would lie with the headmaster, or rather with those who made upthe rules which forced the headmaster to contemplate such a ludicrous step. And ultimately, in a democracy,that s all of us. The headmaster might turn round and say he couldn t get anyone whoreally knew Spanish for love or money. Market forces, again. But what a slur on our society, and those wholeadit.

The governmenthave showntime and again in other spheres thatit is possible to manipulate marketforces. In fact they've spentbillions doing so. Let them do so in the sphere of Mathematics teaching, if we all agree it s so important for the future economic health of the country.
