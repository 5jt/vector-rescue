*by Howard A. Peelle*
{ .byline }

Horner’s method for evaluating a polynomial (see Spunde’s Technical Correspondence, Vector 8.3 (Jan 1992) pp 138-9) can be expressed in J as follows, for (ascending) coefficients `CS` and a single value `V`:

```text
+`*/}.,V,.CS    NB. From Iverson's AN INTRODUCTION TO J p.22
```

A defined verb

```text
Horner =. +`*/ @ }. @ , @ ,.
```

can be used (albeit inefficiently) with a list of values `VS`:

```text
VS Horner"0 1 CS
```

Alternatively, a little verb which adds its left argument to `VS` times its right argument can be inserted in list `CS` all at once:

```text
(+ VS&*)/CS
```

By way of explanation, a taste of J follows:

In the first expression, `,.` is Append (an extension of APL’s Catenate-along-the-last-dimension) which joins `V` to each item in `CS`. The resulting 2-column matrix is Ravelled (`,`) then Beheaded (`}.` does 1 Drop) producing a vector of coefficients interspersed with copies of `V`. (Note that J notation employs some two-character symbols, here, for example, `,.` and `}.` with period suffixes.) Then Insert `/` (same as APL’s Reduction operator with a vector) applies two verbs (functions) to that vector. Plus `+` and Times `*` (× in APL) are Tied (`` ` ``) together as a gerund (akin to a function array) — forming boxed noun equivalents of the verbs (see Bernecky’s "Gerunds and Representations", APL91 ACM).

The J definition of Horner is similar to direct definition in that it uses only one line but uses the copula `=.` (like APL’s `←`) instead of a colon and is "tacit", that is, uses verbs without showing their arguments explicitly. Here the Tied verb `` +`* `` is the argument for Insert (an adverb); the resulting derived verb is the left argument of the Atop conjunction (`@`) and Behead (`}.`) is its right argument. That verb composition is the argument of another Atop `@` with Ravel (`,`) on its right. That, in turn, is finally composed Atop the result of Append (`,.`) applied to the two arguments given to Horner. It might be noted that this definition is equivalent to — but not procedurally identical to — the algorithm above, which executes verb composition in the reverse order. This can be seen by typing the name of the verb (here, Horner) by itself on a J system, which produces a boxed representation showing the nesting of verbs (and adverbs and conjunctions).

Once defined, Horner can, of course, be executed: `V Horner CS`. It can also be executed, as is, with a list of values `VS` by applying the Rank conjunction (`"`) shown here with a right argument of `0 1` indicating each scalar in the left argument to be used with the vector right argument (and the result assembled accordingly). Note that if a noun were given as the right argument (instead of pronoun `CS` here), either it or the right argument to `"` must be parenthesized.

Lastly, consider the alternative of composing a verb which adds its left argument to `VS` times its right argument and inserting that verb between each item of `CS`. `(+ VS&*)` is a "hook" composed of two verbs: Plus (`+`) and a monadic verb composed of `VS` With (`&`) Times (`*`). In general, a dyadic hook `x (f g) y` is `x f g y` and the conjunction `&` bonds its noun argument with its verb argument to derive a monadic verb.

The plum — defining a verb for the last expression of Horner’s method — is left for you, Jack, to pull out.
