*by Michel Dumontier*

## Summary

The two problems announced in the title are linked. To solve the one is to solve the other. On the other hand solving the first leads to the resolution of another problem that had not yet been solved [1] before this present attempt. This article was first published in *Les Nouvelles d’APL* N° 35.

Let A be a matrix of integers of order n, p an integer and V a vector of integers of n elements all less than p. Given `W = p|A+.###V`, is there a matrix B such that `p|B+.###W` restores the original vector V?

The answer is yes, if A is well formed (but not in the way one normally thinks – see [2]). We give here a recipe for the calculation of B under this assumption. We will call B the *inverse modulo p of A* (the term *inverse of A modulo p* would be more ambiguous).

## Introduction

Such a system can be solved manually up to order 3. For any order n, despite reading through a large bibliography and searching the web, nowhere have we found a solution to this problem. As usual, using APL and J tools (that, a long time ago, I christened ‘faitout-vite’ in French, possibly SpeedyGee in English), I found the general solution in one day, first of all in APL. As the numbers grow very quickly, eventually I had to resort to J, which lets one work in extended precision, that is integers without limit. As they say, if you want it done, do it yourself, and I think we should not hesitate when we have tools such as APL and J at our disposal.

The work here presented served as preparation for a paper where the techniques developed are put to a practical application in the exciting field of cryptology. [2]

We will deal in turn first with the APL solution and then the J solution.

The APL solution leads to a deadlock when the matrix A to invert (modulo p) is of a order high enough, because we would need numbers larger than the limit of APL integers. However, we will not pass in silence over this work we had to do.

## 1. APL solution

As we need the integer matrix determinant and inversion, we pick up our dusty old notebooks and write these functions in APL.

Calculation of the adjoint matrix:

```apl
    ∇ Z←ADJOINT A
[1]   Z←⍉COFACTORS A
    ∇
```

Calculation of the cofactors:

```apl
    ∇ CO←COFACTORS A;I;J;⎕IO
[1]   CO←⍳0 ⋄ I←1
[2]   INC:J←0
[3]   IT:J←J+1
[4]   CO←CO,A COFACTOR I,J
[5]   →(J<(⍴A)[1])/IT
[6]   I←I+1
[7]   →(I≤1↑⍴A)/INC
[8]   CO←(⍴A)⍴CO
[9]
    ∇
```

Calculation of a cofactor:

```apl
    ∇ Z←A COFACTOR V;SIGN;⎕IO
[1]   ⎕IO←1 ⋄ SIGN←¯1*+/V
[2]   Z←SIGN×DET A MINOR V
[3]
    ∇
```

Calculation of the determinant:

```apl
    ∇ Z←DET M ;⎕IO
[1]   ⎕IO←1 ⋄ Z←M[1;]+.×(COFACTORS M)[1;]
    ∇
```

Calculation of a minor:

```apl
    ∇ Z←A MINOR V;Q;⎕IO
[1]   ⎕IO←1 ⋄ Q←(V[1]≠⍳(⍴A)[1])/[1]A
[2]   Z←(V[2]≠⍳(⍴A)[2])/Q
[3]
    ∇
```

Calculation of the inverse:

```apl
    ∇ Z←INV M
[1]   →((DET M)=0)/FIN
[2]   Z←(ADJOINT M)÷DET M ⋄ →0
[3]   FIN:'DET =0'
    ∇
```

Thus we are well prepared and are sure that we a re working with integers.

We can now write the function that calculates the inverse modulo p of a matrix.

### Calculation of the inverse matrix modulo p

Taking into account successive vectors from the first to the last line of the unit matrix of order n, we have the next solution. We call it DIO in honour of the Greek mathematician Diophantus.

```apl
    ∇ Z←M DIO P;⎕IO;I;T;U;N;IT;MU;D
[1]   ⎕IO←1⋄ T←⍉⌽M ⋄N←1↑⍴M
[2]   U←(⍳N)∘.=⍳N ⋄ IT←INV T ⋄D←DET M
[3]   MU←⊖U ⋄ Z←(N,N)⍴0 ⋄ I←1
[4]   INC:Z[I;]←(P|D×⍳P)⍳⌊(P|((IT)+.×MU[I;])×D)
[5]   →(N≥I←I+1)/INC
[6]
    ∇
```

(This is the function I searched for everywhere and eventually I had to write for myself as well.)

## 2. J Solution

Let us rewrite our routines.

For the determinant, no sweat:

```j
det=: -/ . *
```

Luckily for us, this train is special-cased in the latest releases of the interpreter produced by Jsoftware and we don’t have to hide the beauty of the mathematical definition behind a thousand lines of efficient, but ugly, code.

J does not have among its primitives the equivalent of APL’s vertical mirror, so let us re-write it in tacit form as:

```j
mv=: |."_1@]
```

We will trust the inverse matrix calculated by the J primitive `%.`

```j
   dio=: 3 : 0
:
t =: |: mv x.
n =:0{$x.
u =: (i.n) =/ i.n
mu=:|.u
it=: %. t
d =: det x.
b =:(n,n)$0
i =:0
while. i<n do.
b =: (y.|d*i.y.) i. (y. |((it)+/ .*(i{mu))*d) (i}) b
i =:i+1
continue.
end.
)
```

We shall not pass over in silence the ulterior motive that some may have had reading this paper…

It’s no accident that we have chosen p=256 because the residue modulo 256 is a number in the range 0 to 255 and can represent the index in origin 0 of any character of a computer in the `⎕AV`.

If b is the inverse modulo p of a, it is necessary to know that it is symmetrical: then a is the inverse modulo p (the same p of course) of b.

Let us verify this by working on the same vector v again but let us use b instead of a as the transformation matrix. Except in case of an unlikely coincidence, we should not have the same w.

```j
   v
234 79 158 41 220 6 242 228 125 56
   +w=:p|b+/ . * v
69 1 205 61 73 100 88 168 186 247
```

The w transformed with a was:

```j
241 162 174 217 22 28 170 135 124 136
```

It does not matter if the w calculated is not the same, we are going to find our v by the magical operation:

```j
   p|a+/ . *w
234 79 158 41 220 6 242 228 125 56
```

One concludes immediately that the matrices a and b can be kept secret separately. Person B for example, who knows how to invert modulo p, sends to his correspondent (A) the matrix a that he can use to transform messages for B’s eyes only. B reconstitutes the messages with b but can send messages to A by using b. A will then reconstitute the message with a and does not need to know b.

Of course what is sent down the wire will not be the numeric vector w but the characters situated at the indices contained in w in the `⎕AV` (or maybe something cleverer – see [2]). In other words, the person A would transmit the message T[w] (reads naturally T indexed by w), where T is the atomic vector in our example.

A last paragraph on an interesting property of the transformation: of course one could take matrices a whose elements are greater than -255 and less than 255 but it would be as if he took the matrix `256|a`.
