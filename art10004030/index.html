A. Introduction¶

A linear function is a monad that distributes over addition. Every scalar linear function is equivalent to multiplication by a constant, but vector and higher rank linear functions prove fruitful and interesting. We begin with examples of rank 1 linear functions; before reading them, the reader might furnish examples of his own:

Comment on the results.

As remarked in the answer to exercise A.1, some linear vector functions are limited to arguments of a fixed dimension, and we will concentrate on such functions. More particularly, we will emphasize uniform functions that produce results of the same dimension as the argument, and are "closed" in the sense that the result of one is a suitable argument to another.

As illustrated in Exercises A.2 and A.3, the matrix product mp=. +/ . * may be used with a matrix m in the expression mp&m to yield a linear function. Conversely, any linear function of fixed dimension can be represented in this form. The matrix required to represent a specific linear function f is obtained by applying it to an identity matrix as illustrated below:

B. Representations and Compositions¶

Since 2 #. 1 1 0 0 is twelve, the list 1 1 0 0 is said to be its base-2 representation. More generally, a list of integers i is said to be the base-b representation of the number b #. i .

Similarly, the coefficients c are said to be the polynomial representation of the function c&p., and m is said to be the (matrix) representation of the linear function mp&m. More specifically, we may call m the post-representation to distinguish it from the pre-representation in the form n&mp .

B.1 Use various integers b and integer lists c to experiment with the functions b&#. , and c&p.

B.2 Test the assertion that the functions +/\ and +/\. are linear, and write and enter expressions to yield their dimension-4 representations.

In maths, notation such as f ∘ g is used to denote the composition of functions f and g, with the definition that f ∘ g x is f g x, We will use the term more generally to refer to the use of several conjunctions (including @ and & and &.) and of forks. For example, if f and g are linear functions, then f + g is also a linear function, and its representation is the sum of the representations of f and g.

B.3 Enter m=. ? 4 4 $9 [ n=. ? 4 4 $9 and f=. mp&m and g=. mp&n to generate two random matrices of dimension 4, and experiment with the uniform linear functions f and g represented by them.

f@g   f+g   f-g   f*g   f%g

D. Scans¶

In Section A it was seen that the prefix scan adverb \ applied to the linear function +/ yielded a linear function. This suggests a further examination of it and other scan adverbs listed in the Vocabulary of the dictionary of J, including the suffix scan (\.), the infix and outfix (dyadic cases of \ and \.), and the oblique (/.) which is of interest for linear functions of rank 2.

Answers to Chapter 2¶

A. Introduction¶

A.1 The first three examples of linear functions apply to vector arguments of any dimension. The rest are restricted to a fixed dimension (4), We are commonly concerned only with a fixed dimension, as in plane geometry (2), solid geometry (3), and space-time (4).

The dimension of the result of a function may differ from the dimension of the argument (as in +/@(1 2 3 4&*)"1 and +/" 1).

A.2 The shape of m (that is, $m) is 3 4 ; the dimension of the result is 3, and the dimension of the argument is 4 .

A.3 Note that the space before the dot in the expression mp=. +/ . * is essential (since /. would otherwise denote an adverb different from /). Also note that the final expressions are tests of linearity, and that the dot conjunction provides for other functions that are similar to the matrix product, but are based upon functions other than summation and multiplication.

A.4 The matrix m must be square, that is, =/$ m.

A.6 Because the function mp&m is an identity.

A.S One is the transpose of the other (n -: |: m). This is true even for non-uniform functions.
